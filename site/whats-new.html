v0.43 18 October 2026 new scene

The navigator that rolls, and a drop that implodes

There is a seventh scene on the page now, on key 7, and the director never picks it — you have to ask for it. It is a copy of the navigator the page opens on, with its whole way of moving thrown out and rebuilt. The navigator you already know walks a hand-drawn map: a short list of interesting places inside the shape, worked out in advance by me, and a route between them. This one carries no map at all. Wherever it happens to be sitting, the shape itself hands it a coordinate — how tightly the orbit there spirals in, and which way it turns — and the music steers in that coordinate directly, which means it can be anywhere in the set and still know which way is out. Loud presses it toward the edge of the lobe it is in, so the spiral tightens as the track opens up; the sung note, or the brightness of the mix when nobody is singing, turns it around the rim; the harmony and the key turn it further; the bass and the kick push it outward on every hit; a section change mirrors the whole picture top to bottom. When the music builds, it slides along the rim until it is sitting on a gateway — the pinch where a smaller bulb is budded onto the one it is in — and crosses through into the smaller one; when the energy falls away again it rolls back out the way it came in. Nothing there is looked up in a table. The shape is asked, every frame, where its own edges and gateways are. The drop goes through the gateway instead of around it. At the pinch itself the orbit neither escapes nor settles — it crawls, and the set's interior opens into petals, which the scene draws as bands of the time an orbit spends before it leaves. On the drop the scene walks to that exact point, holds there while the petals come up, and then steps just outside, into the hair-thin wedge between the two lobes, where the filled set is no longer solid: it comes apart from the inside out, and the dust eats inward as the step grows with the slam and fills back in as it shrinks. The distance breathes with the beat, and how far out it goes is how hard the drop hit. Then it eases back and the ordinary rolling picture returns. Three rounds of retuning came out of watching it on a big screen. It was alive but ten times quieter than the navigator it was cloned from, so it now lives against the rim rather than in the middle, with springs and gains set to a beat rather than a bar, and a grammar of its own: the bass and the kick pulse the spiral's tightness, the lobes turn a little every bar and sway across a phrase so the outline is never the same two bars running, and the snare twitches it from side to side. It was spending too much of the machine on the implosion and the picture went soft during drops, so the implosion's budget now follows the same quality governor as everything else, and the glow that was washing the shape out has been pulled back to the very rim — the boundary and its spiral arms read against a dark interior where before there was a lit blob. And it was wandering too deep: far enough down a chain of bulbs, the set stops looking like the set and becomes a rosette of little circles that barely changes. It now stays home, in the main body and the bulbs attached directly to it, holds a smaller bulb for a couple of bars once the music stops asking for it, and never pins itself flat against the wall. Being honest about it. Nobody has watched this but me, and the eye it has to convince is at home in front of the big screen — that is the whole reason it is on a key of its own and out of the rotation. On real tracks the implosion eats in much more thinly than it does on my test timeline, and the knob for that has not been set per song. Two songs in my library have a drop that the engine does not raise inside the window I measured, so they never implode at all and I do not yet know which of the two detectors is at fault. During a drop this scene costs several times what the home navigator costs, so on a weaker machine the governor will soften the picture for those few seconds. And one song still washes out to a flat blob at the deepest setting it is allowed to reach. The home navigator, on key 3, has not changed by a single byte.

v0.42 17 October 2026 new scene

The cloud follows the singer

Watching the swarm of grains on key 4 with a song I know well, I could not see the singing in it. The kick, the snare and the hat each had a voice in that picture and the singer had a hairline: a quarter of the light moved onto a ring so thin that under a real drum kit you would never find it, and the one thing it did with the melody was slide that ring in and out by less than its own width. The detector was not at fault, and I measured that before I touched anything — on a song the network had never heard it lit through all of the singing and almost none of the rest, and named the note within a semitone nearly every time. The signal was arriving clean and the scene was spending it on nothing. Now the register the singer is in is a band rather than a hairline, lit in the colour of the note being sung and drawn with bigger grains, and the rest of the cloud — the drums' flashes included — gives up nearly half its light to it while a voice is sounding, then takes it all back within a breath of the voice stopping. The drums recede under a held line instead of shouting over it, and the moment the singing stops they are as bright as they ever were. The note is also a height now: the lit band floats up out of the body of the cloud on a high note and sinks under it on a low one, carrying the whole swarm a little with it, in every formation the scene has. The drum grammar underneath is untouched — not one kick, ring or hat moved — this is only a question of who gets the light while somebody sings. One old mistake came out in the washing. The view this engine draws through is mirrored top to bottom, which nothing on screen could ever tell you, and the one place it mattered was the rise: the line the last release lifted for a sung note had been lifting it for a low note and pushing it down for a high one. Both rises are the right way up from here. There is a ninth song in the library, a rock record from the seventies with a real singer over a real band, and it is the first one I added after the voice detector had finished learning — it was deliberately kept out of that learning, so what it says is the only word I have from a song the network cannot have memorised. It says the detector is good. It found the singing wherever the separated vocal track said there was singing, it called the note right nearly every time, and over the whole song it claimed a voice almost nowhere there wasn't one — less often, in fact, than on the solo piano I keep as a control. The song has the widest quiet-to-loud range of anything in the library and a long stretch in the middle with no voice at all, which is exactly the material this scene needed to be judged on. One caveat on it: the engine reads its pulse at double the speed you would nod your head to, and until I have sat and listened to that properly the song is in the library for watching rather than for measuring against. Being honest about the rest. A solo piano sitting in a singer's range can still make the cloud flicker a little; it is a fraction of what it was two releases ago and it is not nothing. A chopped, shouted sample still reads as a voice with no note in it, so the light it makes has no colour. On one song the lit line through the swarm is bright enough to own the picture by itself. And the seam that puts the whole swarm on a line is still a once-a-section decision rather than something a voice can open on its own — on a song that is sung most of the way through, letting a voice open it would throw away the twenty formations the scene has for the sections of a track. So far the eye on all of this is mine; the one it has to convince is at home in front of a big screen.

v0.41 17 October 2026 engine

The voice finds the note, and the ink rests

The detector that listens for singing was rebuilt, and the thing it got better at is the one it was worst at: the note. Before this release it answered a window at a time and guessed the pitch off the whole mix, and on two of the three sung tracks in my library the note it named was wrong more often than it was right — which means the colour a sung line painted was mostly a colour the song was not in. It now answers on every step rather than every window, and the pitch comes from the network itself instead of being chased afterwards. On the tracks with a clear lead vocal it lands on the right note nearly always, and on the hardest of the three — a shouted sample over a synth — it still lands where it used to. You see it in the two scenes that read the voice: on key 4 the lit seam through the swarm of grains takes the colour of the note being sung, and on key 5 so does the strand lifted out of the nest. Hearing a sung line in the right colour is the whole of what changed there. Being honest about the rest of it. The voice still enters a beat behind the note at a walking tempo, and a shade later than it did in the last release — that is the trade for a network that hears further back before it commits, and it is the gate I most wanted to meet and did not. A solo piano sitting in a singer's range still lights the scenes a little; it is a fraction of what it was two releases ago, and it is not nothing. A chopped, shouted sample still reads as a voice being present with no note in it, so the light it makes has no colour. And the network still knows my own library far better than it knows music it has never heard. The second pool of ink, on key 2, has gone back to the reading I kept returning to. The rig of invisible mouths I built for it last release is gone and the pool is fed the way the first one is, which is the version I had on a recording from a week before and could not stop preferring; it is in the page exactly as it was that afternoon, proved frame by frame against the old file rather than rebuilt from memory. One thing goes with that: the pool no longer reads the voice at all, because reading it was the rig's own idea. The grains and the nest carry the voice now, and the first pool never did. Last, the thing that had been quietly taking frames from everything else. The pool of ink is a fluid simulation, and it was being run under every scene on the page whether anything on screen was drawing from it or not — on a machine with graphics power to spare you would never notice, and on one without it that is a real slice of every single frame spent for nobody. It now runs only while a scene that actually draws from it is on screen. On the slowest renderer I have, the dust and the navigator each got better than a tenth of their frame rate back, and that is the dropped frames I went looking for. There is one visible consequence and it is the right one: when a pool scene comes in now, it fills from dark instead of fading up the picture the pool happened to be holding from minutes ago.

v0.40 14 October 2026 engine

The listening moves to its own thread

Everything the page does to hear the music — the drums, the bass, the key it is in, the texture, the pulse and the voice detector that arrived in the last two releases — now runs on a thread of its own, beside the one that draws. Nothing a scene is handed changed, and I proved that rather than claimed it: the old arrangement and the new one were run side by side on the same sound, on every track in my library, and every value a scene reads was compared one at a time, exactly, never within a tolerance — not one of them differed, and the shot-by-shot proofs that pin each scene's pixels all came back the same to the byte. What changed is that the thread that draws no longer stops to listen. Watching a shared tab, which is how I watch nearly all of the time, drawing gets back almost all of the time the listening used to take from it, and more again when the voice detector is doing its work too. On this machine you will not see the detail level move, because that time was margin rather than a step; on a machine nearer its limit it is the difference between a frame arriving and a frame arriving late, and it is the same margin the softness I went looking for last release came out of. Playing a file the way the test harness plays one keeps the listening where it was, because there the sound is handed in and read back inside the same frame, so every pixel proof still runs the path it always ran. If the new thread will not start, the page falls back to the old arrangement whole, with nothing missing. The readout in the corner says which thread is listening and what it costs, and a switch in the address bar forces the old one, so the two can be watched one after the other on the same music — and the new one is the one I would keep. Being honest about what moved and what did not. One thing moves: the part that follows the beat is now told about a kick a hop of the thread after the ears found it, which on everything I measured shifts the tempo it reports, and the place it puts the beat, by less than an eye reads on a beat line — but it is a shift, and it exists only while listening live; the kick itself still carries the moment it happened. And one old limit is worth naming because it now looks like a new one: a single saved page opened straight off the disk cannot start this browser's audio plumbing at all, which was already true of the page before this one — served over the network, the way the site is, it is the real thing.

v0.39 12 October 2026 engine

The voice reaches the scenes

The singing the engine learned to hear last time now reaches two more of the scenes, and the detector itself had a second pass. On key 4, the swarm of grains lights where the voice is: the grains carrying the sung register brighten and take the note's colour, so a held line draws a lit seam through the cloud. On key 5, the nest of circles grows a strand — one fibre lifted out of the bundle, its height set by the pitch, its hue the note being sung, its brightness the voice against the rest of the mix. Both go quiet the moment nobody is singing, and each has a switch in the address bar that turns the read off, so the before and the after can be watched one after the other on the same music. The detector itself was retrained. Its old failing was a confident false alarm: a solo piano or a lead synth sitting in the voice's range read as singing, and on a piano recording it had never heard it lit up on better than a quarter of the frames. It now lights on about one frame in fourteen there, and the other instrumental controls fell with it — a track of synth leads from a seventh of its frames to a twenty-fifth, a drum track to under one in a hundred. That was bought with caution: it also misses more of the quiet singing than it did, about a tenth of what it used to catch, and that is the trade I would rather have — a scene that stays dark through a piano solo is honest, a scene that pulses at a piano is a lie. It is still a small network hearing a third of a second at a time; it still answers about an eighth of a second behind the note, and on music it has never heard it is right about four times in five. A shouted, chopped vocal sample reads as presence with no pitch, so the light it makes has no colour. One bug went with all this, and it turned out to be the camera rather than the sound: on key 5 the voice was quietly nudging the frame on every sung note, which on a vocal line reads as a pump. The voice is now read after the camera has decided where it is looking, so the strand lights and the nest sits still. Nothing is fetched and nothing leaves your device: the network's numbers are part of the page, as before. Two quieter fixes went in with it, both from asking why the picture had gone soft. The part of the engine that decides how much detail to draw was reading the pause while a track is read off the disk as the machine struggling, and then spent the next half-minute climbing back out of a hole that was never there; it now waits out a load and carries on from where it stood, so a scene opens at the detail it was already drawing. And when a file's vocal part was separated beforehand and is the thing feeding the picture, the small network is no longer asked for an answer that is thrown away on the same frame — the only listening being done is the listening you can see.

v0.38 12 October 2026 engine

The engine learns to hear a voice

Retina Rave now listens for singing. A small neural network — about forty thousand numbers, trained on a public corpus of Creative-Commons music annotated for voice and no voice, and shipped inside the page itself — reads the sound as it arrives and answers three questions many times a second: is someone singing, how loud against the rest of the mix, and on what note. The second pool of ink, on key 2, uses all three, and it is a different scene from the one that shipped last time. The band grammar I put there a release ago moved too fast to watch, so it is gone; in its place the pool is fed by a rig of invisible mouths — twenty-three of them, lining the two sides and the top and set symmetrically inside the frame, the way a lighting designer hangs a truss rather than aiming one lamp at the middle. Each part of the music gets its own mouths, and a run across the rig reads as a chase. The voice is now the lead channel: the mouth that draws the melody rides on the singer instead of on a metronome, its height is the pitch — high notes high on the screen — and its colour is the note being sung, taken around the circle of fifths like every other harmony colour here. When nobody is singing, the lead goes quiet rather than inventing a line. The drop no longer throws a shockwave across this pool; the rig answers it instead. The listening costs the picture nothing — the detector runs beside the rest of the ears, in well under a tenth of a millisecond per step, and the weights are part of the page, so nothing is fetched and nothing leaves your device. The on-device promise on the landing page is unchanged. Being honest about what it is: the network is small and hears only a third of a second at a time, so it is quick rather than clever. It knows the eight tracks in my own library well; on music it has never heard, a solo piano or a lead synth sitting in the voice's range can read as a voice on a fifth of the frames, and a pitch taken off a full mix lands within a semitone about three times in four when there is a clear lead vocal, less when the voice is a chopped sample riding over a synth. There is a second, more accurate route that only works on my own machine: playing a file, the page can read a vocal part that was separated offline beforehand, which hears the voice on its own with no mix to confuse it, and a switch in the address bar flips between the two so the difference is visible. A shared tab is always the live network — which is how I watch almost all of the time, and why it was built first. The little readout in the corner says which of the two is feeding the picture.

v0.37 11 October 2026 new scene

Six scenes, six keys, and the arrows

The scene list is down to the six I actually watch, and they sit on the first six number keys: 1 the pool of ink, 2 a second pool that reads the bands of the music, 3 the navigator the page opens on, 4 the dust, 5 the torus, 6 the mandala. The arrow keys now step through them in both directions — right is forward, the same as n, and left goes back — which is what the two buttons in the bottom bar have always done. Eight scenes I never looked at have left the page: the polytopes, the ladder, the first torus, the two older navigators, the waves, the superformula and the sand plate. Nothing was thrown away — they are all still in the repository, frozen at the version they last ran in — and the page that loads is a quarter smaller without them. The second pool, on key 2, is new and experimental: where the first one takes every sound as a splash of ink, this one gives each band of the music its own kind of coupling. The sub-bass is a field that heaves the whole pool instead of a dot, every kick launches a pair of spinning vortices that travel across the screen, the mids decide how stiff the ink is, the highs are the surface — a grain, a dip, a shine — the harmony draws with a pen, and the simulation’s own clock breathes with the sub, drops to half speed in the hollow before a drop and double-steps when it lands. It was tuned on one track, the newest in my library, rather than on all eight, so treat it as a sketch rather than a finished scene: the director never picks it, you have to. And sharing a tab now opens on the pool of ink rather than the navigator, and stays there while it settles.

v0.36 10 October 2026 new scene

The drop as a shockwave — and FLUID joins the rotation

Two things for FLUID, the pool of ink. First, the drop is now a shockwave: on the beat the drop lands, a ring of velocity leaves the centre of the screen and travels outward at the song’s own beat speed, carrying the ink with it — the centre empties physically, the ink piles up at the front, and the drop’s kicks refill the middle over the next beat. How hard it throws is how hard the bass slams back, ranked against the track’s own kicks, so a soft drop is a softer throw. It fires only on a confirmed drop — the bass has to return — so a breakdown that teases never fires it. It works the same from a file and from a live tab capture; on a live night the only difference is the ear that finds the drop. Second, FLUID now takes part in the director’s rotation: it bids for sub-driven passages under a sure key in a dark mix, next to the navigator, the particles and the torus, and stays at least 30 to 90 seconds like every scene. Nothing else moved — every other scene renders exactly as before, frame for frame, and n still cycles to FLUID whenever you want it.

v0.35 9 October 2026 new scene

A pool of ink under every scene — FLUID

There is now a fluid under every scene: a pool of ink that the music stirs, simulated on the graphics card every frame, built after Pavel Dobryakov’s WebGL-Fluid-Simulation (MIT, github.com/PavelDoGreat/WebGL-Fluid-Simulation). The new scene FLUID shows that pool as itself, lit like oil on water — press n to cycle to it, or open the page with #scene=12. Where the ink enters is the note: the bass sits at the song’s key, its fifth to one side, its fourth to the other, the odd notes out at the walls. How hard the music hits is how big the splash: every kick lifts the ink, the snare shears it, the hats sprinkle it, and the loud bars throw more than the quiet ones. The song’s key is the colour, and it stays put once the ears are sure of it. The drop clears the pool — a beat of empty water, then the drop’s kicks refill it. Pads and chords keep a faint floor of ink moving so a quiet passage is never a black screen, sized to the track’s own range. All of this was tuned on every track in the library, one at a time. The other scenes are unchanged: the fluid only reaches a scene that asks for it. And the glitch rows — the screen tear on a drop — can be switched off: press G, or open the page with #glitch=0 for a bookmark that stays off.

v0.34 8 October 2026 new scene

NAV moves with the music

The home scene, NAV, was rebuilt to move with the music. The picture no longer washes out to white at the loudest bars: the brightest moments are now the most detailed, with a dark interior and a bright rim, and white is spent on the snare and the drop. The colours sit on the song’s key — minor keys land on green — and step once per phrase instead of drifting around the wheel. The set jumps on the real kicks, about once a bar, breathes with the beat, presses to the rim when the sub-bass is in and relaxes when it drops out, leans with the bass note, and the exterior filaments turn once per bar instead of twice a beat. At each phrase it walks to a new part of the set instead of sitting in one place for a whole section, preferring busier shapes when the music is loud and calmer ones when it is quiet, and every drop lands on a new ray. The kaleidoscope mirror is off on NAV, so the set itself is what you see. The previous navigator is still a key press away on 9 for anyone who prefers it.
